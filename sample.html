Wedding Day Sunshine

A fictional wedding letter, written for Maya and Jules.

A request from the kitchen door

Dear Maya and Jules,

Of all the reasons anyone has given me for wanting a dry Saturday, yours is the first that involves a dog with a job. Otis has two rings to deliver. Your dad says he’s been practicing with curtain rings in the kitchen. I’ve been trying to imagine the expression on his face when everyone he knows turns up to watch him do the thing he already does perfectly well for a piece of cheese.

Every request for good weather has a size. Your dad measured yours in footsteps. There are twelve from the kitchen door to the lemon tree if you’re Maya, eleven if you’re Jules, and considerably more if you’re Otis and stop twice to sniff. Your dad would like a little sun on those few feet of Sonoma soil. He’d like you to hear one another easily. And when you turn to face your guests, he’d like you to be able to find the empty chair he set out for your grandmother.

He asked me to put that wish into words for you. I like that he knew exactly how much sky to ask for. The rest of the county can put in its own requests. This letter belongs to one garden, one Saturday, and the two people standing in it.

A little room above the lemon tree

If I could write your morning onto a map, I’d begin at the kitchen table. I picture two cups there, a pair of scissors somebody will spend ten minutes looking for, and a paper bag with the good cheese in it. Through the open door you can see the backs of the chairs. From that angle they look oddly ordinary. In a few hours each one will have somebody you love sitting in it, wearing shoes they took some trouble to choose.

Here’s where I would put the first patch of sunshine. On the kitchen floor, just inside that door. Give Otis a warm place to lie while people step over him. Let somebody look down in the middle of a frantic search for the scissors and find a dog so perfectly at home in this commotion that they remember where they left them.

I’d send the next patch slowly across the grass until it reached your grandmother’s chair. There are people at a wedding whose presence makes itself felt through one particular person. Someone sees that chair and can already hear what she would have said about the dog. Someone else remembers when that tree was small. For half a minute, two people who miss her get to look at each other and smile about exactly the same thing. That is the kind of warmth your dad had in mind when he wrote to me.

Finally I’d leave a little light in the branches above you. Just enough to see each other’s faces when the rings arrive. My wish for that part of the afternoon is quite selfish on your behalf. May the person holding a phone decide that one photo will do. May the next minute belong entirely to the people close enough to hear Jules take a breath and Maya start to laugh.

What the garden holds

I keep coming back to the curtain rings. They’re such good stand-ins for wedding rings. Both spend most of their lives doing ordinary work. One lets a person open the curtains in the morning. The other will tap against a mug or leave a faint line in bread dough. For one afternoon your wedding rings will have everybody’s attention. Afterward they’ll go home with you and settle into the thousand small things your hands already do for each other.

If you keep one souvenir of the rehearsal, I hope it’s a curtain ring. Put it somewhere pleasantly unimportant, perhaps with the spare keys. One wet Tuesday, when one of you is looking for something else, a little brass circle will bring back a kitchen, a waiting dog, and your dad counting footsteps out loud because he had decided the sky needed precise instructions.

He put the chair in the garden for a similar reason, I think. It gives a memory an ordinary place to sit. You can glance over as often or as little as you like. You can leave a flower there, or ask someone to straighten it, or give it no particular duty beyond being there. Whoever straightens it gets to do one more small thing for someone they loved. There is room in the day for that, just as there is room for a dog to take the long way round.

Before the guests come out

If you can steal two minutes on Saturday, stand together at the kitchen door. Choose one thing in the garden that you want the other person to see. Point it out. This can be gloriously unromantic. Perhaps one chair has sunk an inch into the lawn and its neighbor looks inclined to follow. The point is to see the garden together once before everyone else comes out and tells you how beautiful it looks.

Then try these words, if they sound like something you’d say. “Of all the places we could have done this, I’m glad we’re here.” Add your own reason. Maya gets one. Jules gets one. You can keep both between yourselves. A wedding asks you to say enough things into a microphone.

If rain does bring everyone inside, you still have the kitchen door. Stand there for a moment and watch the drops leave dark spots on the path. You might take your two minutes then. The view from that doorway will still be the one your dad described when he asked for this letter. And should the clouds lift just as you finish speaking, you have my wholehearted permission to give Otis the credit.

There’s one favor you could ask of your dad afterwards. When he has a minute, have him write down the first thing he saw as you came through the door. Give him the back of this page. His sentence might be about your faces or a shoelace or the fact that he had the cheese in the wrong pocket. Let him choose. Years from now you’ll be able to stand at that door again, turn the page over, and see the two of you coming down the path from where he stood.

A wish to take to the tree

Later, when people ask how your wedding went, you’ll get to choose which version to tell. Your dad may tell it in footsteps. Someone from the back row will tell it in terms of what the dog did. One of you may remember almost nothing between stepping out of the kitchen and feeling that new ring on your hand. I hope you collect all those versions. Someone else will have seen the moment you were too happy to notice.

In the meantime, here is the piece of the letter small enough to carry outside.

May a little sun find your twelve steps to the lemon tree. May the rings arrive by whatever route Otis chooses. And when you reach for each other, may you have one quiet second to feel your hands meet.

With love from the front row, Dad
